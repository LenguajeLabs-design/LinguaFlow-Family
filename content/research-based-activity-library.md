# LinguaFlow Family — Research-Based Activity Library

**Status: DRAFT — Batch 1 of 3 (Review of existing activities + Ages 6–8)**
Ages 9–11 and 12–14, plus the Strongest 12 synthesis, evidence patterns, gaps, and full source list, will be added in subsequent batches and merged into this file.

## 1. Executive summary

This library proposes a set of family activities that let parents support their children's English development through ordinary family life — no worksheets, no teaching expertise, and no requirement to set aside the home language. Each activity is grounded in a credible source (peer-reviewed research, WIDA/Colorín Colorado-type family and EL resources, or established literacy organizations) and is written so a parent could pick it up and use it in one read.

This batch covers the review of LinguaFlow Family's six existing activities and ten new activities for ages 6–8.

## 2. Research method

Sources were prioritized in this order: peer-reviewed research and meta-analyses; WIDA, Colorín Colorado, and Reading Rockets family/EL resources; university reading research centers (e.g., Iowa Reading Research Center); and established literacy organizations. Commercial tutoring sites, generic parenting blogs, and unsourced activity round-ups were excluded. Where an activity is a direct application of a studied intervention (e.g., dialogic reading, echo reading), it is marked **Direct research support**. Where it applies a well-evidenced principle (e.g., open-ended questioning, vocabulary-in-context) to a new family routine, it is marked **Adapted from a research-supported principle**. Where the practice is common professional guidance without a specific controlled study behind this exact activity, it is marked **Established practitioner recommendation**.

## 3. Review of the six existing activities

### 1. Picture Talk — **Retain**
Strong evidence base. This is a family-facing version of dialogic reading, one of the most studied interactive-reading practices, and research specifically extends it to English language learners. Recommend keeping the activity largely as-is, but make sure instructions explicitly invite predicting, connecting to the child's life, and open-ended "what do you notice" questions rather than only naming pictures — that's the piece the research says drives the biggest gains. <cite index="7-1">Dialogic reading has been extended beyond its original preschool use to other ages and populations, including English language learners</cite>.

### 2. Word Hunt — **Revise**
Useful as a noticing game, but if it currently runs as spotting/matching isolated words, the evidence base is weaker than the other five activities. <cite index="37-1">Children are not likely to learn new vocabulary from a list-style exercise stripped of meaningful context; purposeful conversation about words in the context of real experience builds word knowledge instead</cite>. Recommend revising so the "hunt" happens inside a real activity (a walk, the fridge, a bag being packed) and always pairs the word with a short conversation about it, not just identification.

### 3. Would You Rather? — **Retain**
This is a strong, low-prep way to generate open-ended talk. <cite index="37-1">Open-ended questions — ones that require more than a one-word or yes/no answer — are a key way to stimulate the kind of conversation that builds language and vocabulary</cite>. Recommend pairing it with a reminder to pause after asking (see Lesson 2 in the Parent Academy) since <cite index="18-1">giving children time to think before responding leads to more high-quality answers</cite>.

### 4. Echo Reading — **Retain**
Directly evidence-based for exactly this audience. <cite index="29-1">Research shows that echo reading with a fluent adult model improves reading accuracy on familiar passages, and this benefits English learners specifically</cite>. Keep as-is; consider adding a note that it works in any language the family reads together, not only English.

### 5. Kitchen Describer — **Retain**
Well-aligned with oral language research. <cite index="44-1">Hearing and using words in a meaningful context — during everyday moments like cooking, playing, or riding in the car — combined with rephrasing and extending what the child says, builds vocabulary</cite>. Keep, and make sure prompts model the "rephrase and extend" move, not just naming ingredients.

### 6. Family Journal — **Revise**
Good concept, but if it currently asks children to write cold, it's skipping the step the evidence says matters most. <cite index="47-1">In a study of six-year-olds, the ideas and content of a story mattered enormously to written retelling quality, and children produced far more when they told a story aloud first than when they wrote it</cite>. Recommend revising so every entry starts with the child telling the entry out loud, in any language, before writing anything.

## 4. Activity records — Ages 6–8

### 1. Picture Walk Before We Read

**Parent promise:** Before reading a book together, spend two minutes just looking at the pictures and guessing what might happen — it makes the actual reading richer and easier to follow.

**Recommended ages:** 6–8

**Time:** 5 minutes

**Primary focus:** Reading comprehension

**Secondary focus:** Speaking

**Setting:** At home; books

**Materials:** Any picture book

**Steps:**
1. Flip through the pictures without reading the words yet.
2. Ask, "What do you think is happening here?"
3. Ask, "What do you think will happen next?"
4. Then read the story together.

**Example:** Before reading a new library book, a parent and child flip through five pages, guessing what the dog on the cover is looking for.

**Parent phrase:** "What do you notice in this picture?"

**Home-language option:** Do the whole picture walk in the family's strongest language, even if the book itself is in English — the thinking transfers when the child reads the words.

**Starting variation:** Child points and names what they see.

**Growing variation:** Child guesses what might happen next.

**Extending variation:** Child compares this book's pictures to another book or a real experience.

**Why it helps:** This is a family version of dialogic reading, a well-studied practice where adult and child talk through a book together instead of just reading it straight through. <cite index="7-1">The approach was originally developed for picture books and has since been extended to other ages and to English language learners</cite>. Predicting before reading gives children a mental structure to hang the story on, which makes new vocabulary and events easier to follow.

**Evidence classification:** Direct research support

**Sources:**
- Dialogic Reading: Having a Conversation about Books — Reading Rockets / Iowa Reading Research Center, 2017 — https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/comprehension/articles/dialogic-reading-having-conversation-about-books — Explains dialogic reading and its extension to English language learners.

**Cautions:** None significant. Keep it short — this is a warm-up, not a quiz.

### 2. Retell It Your Way

**Parent promise:** After finishing a story, ask your child to tell it back in their own words — it's one of the simplest ways to strengthen both understanding and future writing.

**Recommended ages:** 6–8

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Primary focus:** Reading comprehension

**Secondary focus:** Speaking

**Setting:** At home; books

**Materials:** Any story, or none (a movie or an event works too)

**Steps:**
1. Finish a story together.
2. Ask your child to tell you what happened, start to finish.
3. Ask one "then what happened?" if they stop early.
4. Thank them for the retelling — no correcting needed.

**Example:** After finishing a chapter, a child retells it to a grandparent on a video call in Korean.

**Parent phrase:** "Tell me the story like I've never heard it before."

**Home-language option:** Retelling works in any language — it's the storytelling structure, not the specific vocabulary, that carries over.

**Starting variation:** Child retells with the book open, pointing to pictures as reminders.

**Growing variation:** Child retells with the book closed, in order.

**Extending variation:** Child retells and adds their own opinion about the ending or a character's choice.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="52-1">Research has found that narrative skill at school entry predicts writing and reading comprehension years later</cite>, and <cite index="47-1">children produce far more content and detail when they tell a story aloud than when they write it</cite>. Regular retelling practice builds the same muscle children later use for school writing.

**Evidence classification:** Direct research support

**Sources:**
- When children's storytelling says so much more — Research Outreach, 2023 — https://researchoutreach.org/articles/childrens-storytelling-says-so-much-more/ — Summarizes research linking early narrative skill to later reading and writing outcomes.
- Comparing young children's oral and written story retelling — Taylor & Francis (Cogent Education), 2024 — https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2050571X.2024.2357450 — Study showing oral retells contain more content than written ones at age six.

**Cautions:** None significant.

### 3. Echo Along

**Parent promise:** Read a line, have your child repeat it back the same way — it's a quick way to build reading confidence without any grammar teaching.

**Recommended ages:** 6–8

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Primary focus:** Reading

**Secondary focus:** Listening

**Setting:** At home; books

**Materials:** Any book at a comfortable level

**Steps:**
1. Read one sentence aloud, at a normal, natural pace.
2. Have your child repeat it back, trying to match your rhythm.
3. Move to the next sentence.
4. Stop after a page or two — short bursts work best.

**Example:** A parent reads "The fox ran quickly through the tall grass," and the child echoes it back before they move to the next line.

**Parent phrase:** "Now you try — say it just like I did."

**Home-language option:** This works with a book in the family's language too, and builds the same fluency habits.

**Starting variation:** Echo short phrases instead of full sentences.

**Growing variation:** Echo full sentences with expression.

**Extending variation:** Child reads the next new sentence solo, then checks it with a parent.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="29-1">Research shows that echo reading with a fluent adult model improves reading accuracy on familiar passages, and this strategy is particularly useful for English learners at lower proficiency levels</cite>. Hearing the parent's model first lowers the pressure of reading aloud alone.

**Evidence classification:** Direct research support

**Sources:**
- 4 Techniques for Teaching Reading Fluency to English Learners — Brookes Publishing Blog, 2024 — https://blog.brookespublishing.com/4-techniques-for-teaching-reading-fluency-to-english-learners/ — Describes echo reading's evidence base for English learners.

**Cautions:** Keep sessions brief; echo reading can feel repetitive if stretched too long.

### 4. Name Five Things

**Parent promise:** Wherever you are, pick a spot and name five things you both see — a fast, no-prep way to build vocabulary in context instead of from a list.

**Recommended ages:** 6–8

**Time:** 5 minutes

**Primary focus:** Vocabulary

**Setting:** Any setting

**Materials:** Nothing needed

**Steps:**
1. Pick a spot — a room, a bus stop, a park bench.
2. Take turns naming things you see.
3. For one item each, add a describing word ("big," "shiny," "bent").
4. Ask, "What else could we call that?"

**Example:** Waiting at a bus stop, a family names five things — bench, sign, tree, bicycle, cloud — then describes the cloud as "puffy."

**Parent phrase:** "What else could we call that?"

**Home-language option:** Name things in the home language first, then in English — both count.

**Starting variation:** Point and name single words.

**Growing variation:** Add one describing word to each item.

**Extending variation:** Compare two items ("Which one is heavier? Why?").

**Why it helps:** <cite index="44-1">Children build vocabulary by hearing and using words in meaningful, everyday contexts rather than through isolated drills</cite>, and <cite index="37-1">purposeful conversation about words, embedded in real experience, is what builds durable word knowledge — not word lists</cite>. This activity replaces list-based vocabulary drilling with real, situated naming.

**Evidence classification:** Adapted from a research-supported principle

**Sources:**
- Oral Language: Expanding Your Child's Vocabulary — Reading Rockets — https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/oral-language/articles/oral-language-expanding-your-childs-vocabulary — Describes building vocabulary through everyday meaningful contexts and rephrasing.

**Cautions:** None significant.

### 5. What's the Difference?

**Parent promise:** Hold up two similar objects and ask your child how they're different — it's a simple way to build the comparing-and-explaining language school will ask for later.

**Recommended ages:** 6–8

**Time:** 5 minutes

**Primary focus:** Vocabulary

**Secondary focus:** Explaining and thinking

**Setting:** At home; any setting

**Materials:** Two similar household objects

**Steps:**
1. Grab two similar objects (two spoons, two shoes, two fruits).
2. Ask, "How are these the same? How are they different?"
3. Let your child answer in any language or with gestures.
4. Ask "why do you think that is?" for one answer.

**Example:** Comparing a green apple and a red apple at snack time — same shape, different color and taste.

**Parent phrase:** "How are these different?"

**Home-language option:** Comparisons work in any language; the reasoning skill transfers.

**Starting variation:** Point to what's different.

**Growing variation:** Say a full sentence describing the difference.

**Extending variation:** Explain why the difference might matter (e.g., "the sour one is better for baking").

**Why it helps:** <cite index="37-1">Open-ended questions that ask for more than a one-word answer are one of the clearest ways to prompt the kind of talk that grows vocabulary and language</cite>. Comparing objects is a low-pressure, concrete way to practice this kind of explaining talk before children need it for academic writing.

**Evidence classification:** Adapted from a research-supported principle

**Sources:**
- Developing Vocabulary Through Purposeful, Strategic Conversations — International Literacy Association / Southington Public Schools reprint — https://www.southingtonschools.org/uploaded/faculty/psmolinski/Developing_Vocabulary.pdf — Explains the role of open-ended questions and purposeful conversation in vocabulary growth.

**Cautions:** None significant.

### 6. Ask It a New Way

**Parent promise:** Swap "How was school?" for one specific question — it's an easy fix for the one-word answers most parents dread.

**Recommended ages:** 6–8

**Time:** 5 minutes

**Primary focus:** Speaking

**Setting:** Any setting

**Materials:** Nothing needed

**Steps:**
1. Pick one specific question instead of a broad one ("What made you laugh today?" instead of "How was school?").
2. Ask it, then wait quietly for several seconds.
3. If your child answers briefly, say "tell me more" instead of asking a new question.
4. Share your own answer to the same question too.

**Example:** Instead of "How was school?", a parent asks "What was the easiest part of your day?" and waits.

**Parent phrase:** "Tell me more."

**Home-language option:** Ask in whichever language lets your child explain most fully.

**Starting variation:** Offer two choices to pick from if a full answer feels hard.

**Growing variation:** Ask the open question and wait for a sentence.

**Extending variation:** Follow up by asking your child to compare today with yesterday.

**Why it helps:** Broad questions are surprisingly hard to answer; specific ones are not. <cite index="18-1">Asking open-ended questions that require more than a one-word response, and then giving children time to think before speaking, leads to longer and higher-quality answers</cite>. <cite index="16-1">Multilingual students in particular need extra time to process a question, translate it internally, and build the courage to answer in English</cite>.

**Evidence classification:** Adapted from a research-supported principle

**Sources:**
- Why Wait? The Importance of Wait Time in Developing Young Students' Language and Vocabulary Skills — Wasik, The Reading Teacher (Wiley), 2018 — https://ila.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/trtr.1730 — On open-ended questions plus wait time building language and vocabulary.
- Wait Time — Colorín Colorado — https://www.colorincolorado.org/teaching-ells/ell-classroom-strategy-library/wait-time — Explains why multilingual learners especially benefit from extra processing time before responding.

**Cautions:** None significant.

### 7. Story in Our Language, Every Night

**Parent promise:** Reading or telling a story in your family's strongest language builds the same skills as reading in English — and it's often richer.

**Recommended ages:** 6–8

**Time:** 10–15 minutes

**Primary focus:** Listening

**Secondary focus:** Reading comprehension

**Setting:** At home; books

**Materials:** Any book, or no book at all (a told story works too)

**Steps:**
1. Choose a book in the family's strongest language, or tell a story from memory.
2. Pause once or twice to ask what your child thinks will happen.
3. Let your child ask questions in any language.
4. Talk briefly about how the story connects to your family's life.

**Example:** A grandmother tells a folk tale in Tagalog before bed, pausing to ask what the fox will do next.

**Parent phrase:** "What do you think happens next?"

**Home-language option:** This activity is built entirely around the home language — no English required.

**Starting variation:** Simple, familiar story with pictures.

**Growing variation:** Longer story with a pause-and-predict routine.

**Extending variation:** Child retells the story back the next day.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="24-1">A learner's proficiency in their first language positively supports the development of their second language — skills and concepts learned in one language transfer to another</cite>. Rich storytelling in the home language builds vocabulary, narrative structure, and comprehension skills that carry over into English rather than competing with it.

**Evidence classification:** Direct research support

**Sources:**
- Linguistic Interdependence and the Educational Development of Bilingual Children — Cummins, Review of Educational Research / SAGE, 1979 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543049002222 — Foundational study on how first-language skill transfers to and supports second-language development.

**Cautions:** None significant — this activity actively counters the misconception that home-language use slows English development.

### 8. Grocery List Mission

**Parent promise:** Hand your child part of the shopping list — reading real words for a real reason builds vocabulary faster than flashcards ever could.

**Recommended ages:** 6–8

**Time:** 15–20 minutes

**Primary focus:** Vocabulary

**Secondary focus:** Reading

**Setting:** Shopping or errands

**Materials:** A written or drawn shopping list

**Steps:**
1. Write (or draw, for early readers) three to five items on a card.
2. Give your child the job of finding those items.
3. Ask them to describe where they found each one.
4. Check off items together at the end.

**Example:** A child is given "apples, bread, milk" and reports back that the bread was "on the tall shelf near the door."

**Parent phrase:** "Where did you find it?"

**Home-language option:** Write the list in the home language, in English, or both side by side.

**Starting variation:** Use pictures instead of words for pre-readers.

**Growing variation:** Written words with one describing detail reported back.

**Extending variation:** Child compares prices or sizes of two similar products.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="44-1">Vocabulary grows when words are encountered in meaningful, real contexts</cite>, and a shopping trip supplies real objects, real language, and a real reason to use new words — the combination research points to as more effective than memorized lists.

**Evidence classification:** Adapted from a research-supported principle

**Sources:**
- Oral Language: Expanding Your Child's Vocabulary — Reading Rockets — https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/oral-language/articles/oral-language-expanding-your-childs-vocabulary — On vocabulary growth through meaningful, everyday contexts.

**Cautions:** Keep lists short so the errand doesn't turn into a chore.

### 9. Guess My Object

**Parent promise:** Describe an object without naming it and see if your child can guess it — a playful way to practice the describing language school will ask for.

**Recommended ages:** 6–8

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Primary focus:** Vocabulary

**Secondary focus:** Explaining and thinking

**Setting:** At home; any setting

**Materials:** Household objects

**Steps:**
1. Pick an object without showing it.
2. Give three clues describing it (size, color, use, texture).
3. Let your child guess.
4. Switch roles so your child describes one too.

**Example:** "It's small, it's cold, and you use it to keep drinks cold" — a child guesses "ice cube."

**Parent phrase:** "Can you describe it without telling me what it is?"

**Home-language option:** Give clues in the home language, guess in either language.

**Starting variation:** One-word clues (color, size).

**Growing variation:** Full-sentence clues.

**Extending variation:** Child gives clues about a more abstract idea (a feeling, a season).

**Why it helps:** Describing an object without naming it forces a child to reach for varied vocabulary and organize their thinking — <cite index="37-1">the same purposeful, conversational use of words in context that research links to stronger word knowledge</cite>, in a format that feels like a game rather than a lesson.

**Evidence classification:** Adapted from a research-supported principle

**Sources:**
- Developing Vocabulary Through Purposeful, Strategic Conversations — International Literacy Association / Southington Public Schools reprint — https://www.southingtonschools.org/uploaded/faculty/psmolinski/Developing_Vocabulary.pdf — On vocabulary growth through purposeful conversation rather than list memorization.

**Cautions:** None significant.

### 10. Family Story Swap

**Parent promise:** Trade a short story from your own childhood for one from your child's day — it builds language and strengthens their sense of belonging at the same time.

**Recommended ages:** 6–8

**Time:** 10–15 minutes

**Primary focus:** Family stories and identity

**Secondary focus:** Speaking

**Setting:** Any setting; mealtime

**Materials:** Nothing needed

**Steps:**
1. Tell a short, true story from your own childhood.
2. Ask your child to tell a story back — about their day, a memory, or something funny that happened.
3. Ask one follow-up question ("then what happened?").
4. Thank them for sharing, without correcting the telling.

**Example:** A parent tells about getting lost at a market as a child; their child tells about getting lost finding their classroom on the first day of school.

**Parent phrase:** "Tell me the whole story, from the beginning."

**Home-language option:** Tell stories in whichever language makes the memory come alive — this is about connection and narrative skill, not English practice.

**Starting variation:** Child tells a very short story (a few sentences).

**Growing variation:** Child tells a story with a clear beginning, middle, and end.

**Extending variation:** Child compares their story to the parent's — what was similar or different?

**Why it helps:** <cite index="52-1">Narrative skill at school entry predicts children's later reading comprehension and writing performance</cite>, and family storytelling gives children low-pressure, high-motivation practice at exactly this skill, while reinforcing that their own experiences and languages are worth sharing.

**Evidence classification:** Direct research support

**Sources:**
- When children's storytelling says so much more — Research Outreach, 2023 — https://researchoutreach.org/articles/childrens-storytelling-says-so-much-more/ — On the link between children's oral storytelling ability and later reading/writing outcomes.

**Cautions:** None significant.

## 5. Activity records — Ages 9–11

### 11. Explain It Like a Teacher

**Parent promise:** Ask your child to teach you something they learned at school today — explaining out loud is one of the best ways to build the academic language school requires.

**Recommended ages:** 9–11

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Primary focus:** Explaining and thinking

**Secondary focus:** School confidence

**Setting:** At home; any setting

**Materials:** Nothing needed

**Steps:**
1. Ask your child to pick one thing they learned that day.
2. Ask them to explain it to you as if you know nothing about it.
3. Ask one genuine "why" or "how" follow-up.
4. Thank them — you don't need to evaluate whether it was "correct."

**Example:** A child explains how photosynthesis works while helping set the table, using their hands to show "the plant taking in light."

**Parent phrase:** "Explain it to me like I've never heard of it."

**Home-language option:** Let your child explain in whichever language makes the explanation clearest — understanding the idea matters more than which language carries it.

**Starting variation:** Explain with drawings or gestures alongside a few words.

**Growing variation:** Explain in two or three connected sentences.

**Extending variation:** Explain, then answer a "what if" question that extends the idea.

**Why it helps:** Conversational fluency in a language develops quickly, but the more abstract language needed for school subjects takes years longer to build. <cite index="73-1">Academic language is abstract and context-reduced, and developing it requires learners to practice skills like comparing, classifying, and explaining, not just conversing</cite>. Regularly asking a child to explain what they learned gives them low-stakes practice at exactly this kind of language.

**Evidence classification:** Adapted from a research-supported principle

**Sources:**
- What are BICS and CALP? — Colorín Colorado — https://www.colorincolorado.org/faq/what-are-bics-and-calp — Explains the distinction between conversational and academic language and the skills academic language requires.

**Cautions:** None significant. Resist the urge to fact-check or correct the explanation — the goal is the explaining, not accuracy.

### 12. Three Questions Before I Help

**Parent promise:** Before jumping in on homework, ask three questions instead — children do better when parents guide rather than take over.

**Recommended ages:** 9–11

**Time:** 10–15 minutes

**Primary focus:** School confidence

**Secondary focus:** Explaining and thinking

**Setting:** At home

**Materials:** The homework itself

**Steps:**
1. Ask, "What is the task asking you to do?"
2. Ask, "What do you already understand about it?"
3. Ask, "What's one next step you could try?"
4. Step back and let your child attempt that step before helping further.

**Example:** A child stuck on a reading response first explains the question in their own words, then identifies the one paragraph they're unsure about, before asking for help with just that part.

**Parent phrase:** "Show me where you feel confident, and where you feel stuck."

**Home-language option:** Ask the three questions in the home language even if the homework itself is in English — understanding the task matters more than the language used to discuss it.

**Starting variation:** Parent reads the task aloud and helps identify the first step.

**Growing variation:** Child answers all three questions independently before starting.

**Extending variation:** Child explains their finished work back to a parent afterward.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="76-1">Research summarized by the APA found that direct homework help had a small negative impact on achievement, while parent involvement that builds motivation and engagement had a positive one</cite>, and <cite index="78-1">when an adult takes over a task rather than scaffolding it, children are less likely to persist on similar tasks afterward</cite>. Guiding questions keep the parent involved and supportive without doing the thinking for the child.

**Evidence classification:** Direct research support

**Sources:**
- Does Parent Involvement Really Help Students? Here's What the Research Says — EdWeek, summarizing an APA study, 2023 — https://www.edweek.org/leadership/does-parent-involvement-really-help-students-heres-what-the-research-says/2023/07 — On the limited/negative effect of direct homework help versus other forms of involvement.
- Parental Intrusive Homework Support and Math Achievement: Does the Child's Mindset Matter? — PMC, 2024 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10835763/ — On adults "taking over" tasks reducing children's persistence, versus scaffolded support.

**Cautions:** This is guidance for engagement style, not a claim that parents should never help — some homework genuinely requires direct assistance, especially with instructions in a language parents are less confident in.

### 13. Talk It Out Before You Write It

**Parent promise:** Before your child starts a piece of writing, have them tell you the idea out loud first — spoken ideas are almost always richer than what ends up on the page.

**Recommended ages:** 9–11

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Primary focus:** Writing

**Secondary focus:** Speaking

**Setting:** At home

**Materials:** Paper and pencil (after the talking part)

**Steps:**
1. Ask your child what they plan to write about.
2. Ask them to tell you the whole idea out loud, start to finish.
3. Ask "what else?" once.
4. Have them jot three key words or ideas before writing.

**Example:** Before writing a book report, a child tells their parent the whole plot and their opinion of the ending, then jots down three points to include.

**Parent phrase:** "Tell me your idea first — we'll write it down after."

**Home-language option:** The talking-through step works in any language; the ideas transfer even if the final piece must be written in English.

**Starting variation:** Child tells the idea in a few simple sentences.

**Growing variation:** Child tells a full idea with a beginning, middle, and end.

**Extending variation:** Child explains why they chose to organize the piece the way they did.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="47-1">A study of six-year-olds found that oral retellings contained significantly more words and detail than the same content written down, and that the ideas generated orally strongly predicted the quality of the written version</cite>. Talking through an idea first gives children more raw material to draw from once they start writing.

**Evidence classification:** Direct research support

**Sources:**
- Comparing young children's oral and written story retelling — Taylor & Francis (Cogent Education), 2024 — https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2050571X.2024.2357450 — On oral idea generation outperforming written output and predicting writing quality.

**Cautions:** None significant.

### 14. Model, Don't Correct

**Parent promise:** When your child makes a language mistake, repeat their sentence back correctly instead of pointing out the error — it works better and keeps the conversation going.

**Recommended ages:** 9–11

**Time:** No set time — an everyday habit

**Primary focus:** Speaking

**Setting:** Any setting

**Materials:** Nothing needed

**Steps:**
1. Notice a language mistake in conversation.
2. Respond to the meaning of what your child said first.
3. Naturally repeat their sentence back with the correction, without announcing it.
4. Keep the conversation moving — no need to ask them to repeat it back.

**Example:** A child says "I goed to the library," and a parent responds, "Oh, you went to the library — what did you find?"

**Parent phrase:** "I understand your idea. Tell me more."

**Home-language option:** This same modeling technique works in the home language too, if the family wants to reinforce home-language grammar.

**Starting variation:** Simple one-word or short-phrase corrections modeled back.

**Growing variation:** Full-sentence recasts woven into natural conversation.

**Extending variation:** No direct extension — this is a low-key, ongoing habit rather than a graded activity.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="62-1">A recast repeats what a child said but adds or corrects a piece of language, without interrupting the flow of conversation</cite>, and <cite index="62-1">a systematic review and meta-analysis found recasting to be an effective language intervention technique for children</cite>. Because it doesn't interrupt or call out the mistake, it keeps children willing to keep talking.

**Evidence classification:** Direct research support

**Sources:**
- Recasting as an Intervention for Language Development — EJ Therapy, summarizing Cleave et al. (2015), American Journal of Speech-Language Pathology — https://www.ejtherapy.com/blog/recasting-intervention — Summarizes a systematic review and meta-analysis on the effectiveness of recasting.

**Cautions:** Recasting supports grammar over time; it isn't an instant fix, and it shouldn't be turned into an obvious correction ritual that makes a child self-conscious.

### 15. Two Sides, One Decision

**Parent promise:** Talk through a real family decision together, weighing the options out loud — it's genuine practice at the kind of reasoning language school will expect.

**Recommended ages:** 9–11

**Time:** 10–15 minutes

**Primary focus:** Explaining and thinking

**Secondary focus:** Speaking

**Setting:** Any setting

**Materials:** Nothing needed

**Steps:**
1. Pick a real, low-stakes family decision (what to cook, where to go this weekend).
2. Ask your child to name one option and one reason for it.
3. Ask, "What's a reason someone might disagree?"
4. Decide together, out loud.

**Example:** Deciding between two weekend outings, a child argues for the park because "we haven't been outside all week," while a parent raises that rain is expected.

**Parent phrase:** "What's a reason someone might see it differently?"

**Home-language option:** This kind of reasoning talk works in any language the family is comfortable debating in.

**Starting variation:** Child gives one reason for their choice.

**Growing variation:** Child gives a reason and considers one counterpoint.

**Extending variation:** Child weighs two or three factors before deciding.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="86-1">A study training 9- to 10-year-olds to use more explicit reasoning language during group talk led to a marked shift in how they used language together and improved their performance on a reasoning test</cite>. Everyday family decisions are a natural, repeatable setting for the same kind of talk.

**Evidence classification:** Direct research support

**Sources:**
- Children's Talk and the Development of Reasoning in the Classroom — Mercer, British Educational Research Journal (Wiley), 1999 — https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0141192990250107 — Study of 9- to 10-year-olds showing explicit reasoning-talk training improved children's language use and reasoning performance.

**Cautions:** Keep the stakes genuinely low — this should feel like being included in a family conversation, not being tested.

### 16. Real-World Word Bank

**Parent promise:** Keep a running, no-pressure list of interesting words your child hears in real life — it builds vocabulary the way research says actually works.

**Recommended ages:** 9–11

**Time:** 5 minutes

**Primary focus:** Vocabulary

**Setting:** Any setting

**Materials:** A notebook, phone note, or piece of paper on the fridge

**Steps:**
1. Whenever an interesting word comes up in conversation, a show, or a book, jot it down together.
2. Ask, "Where did we hear that? What do you think it means?"
3. Try using the word again sometime that week.
4. Let the list grow — no quizzes.

**Example:** After hearing "exhausted" in a movie, a family adds it to their list and a child uses it that night: "I'm exhausted from swim practice."

**Parent phrase:** "Where did we hear that word? Let's use it again this week."

**Home-language option:** Add words in any language the family notices — bilingual entries are welcome.

**Starting variation:** Just notice and say the word aloud.

**Growing variation:** Guess the meaning from context before checking.

**Extending variation:** Use the new word in an original sentence about something in the child's own life.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="37-1">Children build lasting word knowledge through multiple, meaningful exposures to words nested in real conversation, not through word lists learned in isolation</cite>. A running "noticed words" list captures vocabulary at the moment it's meaningful, rather than presenting it cold.

**Evidence classification:** Adapted from a research-supported principle

**Sources:**
- Developing Vocabulary Through Purposeful, Strategic Conversations — International Literacy Association / Southington Public Schools reprint — https://www.southingtonschools.org/uploaded/faculty/psmolinski/Developing_Vocabulary.pdf — On vocabulary growth through multiple meaningful exposures versus list-based learning.

### 19. Show Your Thinking Out Loud

### 21. Summarize the Article

**Parent promise:** After your teen reads something — an article, a chapter, a set of instructions — ask for a two-sentence summary. It's one of the most powerful comprehension skills there is.

**Recommended ages:** 12–14

**Time:** 10 minutes

**Primary focus:** Reading comprehension

**Secondary focus:** Academic-language thinking

**Setting:** At home; any setting

**Materials:** Any text — an article, a chapter, instructions

**Steps:**
1. Your teen reads something short.
2. Ask for a summary in two sentences, no more.
3. Ask, "What did you leave out, and why?"
4. That's it — no quiz on details.

**Example:** After reading a short news article, a teen summarizes it in two sentences and explains they left out a statistic because it wasn't the main point.

**Parent phrase:** "Sum it up in two sentences."

**Home-language option:** Summarize in whichever language captures the ideas most efficiently — the skill of identifying what matters transfers across languages.

**Starting variation:** Summarize with a sentence starter ("This is mainly about...").

**Growing variation:** Two clear sentences, no starter needed.

**Extending variation:** Summarize, then explain how this connects to something else they know.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="105-1">A major review of over 200 studies on reading comprehension strategies found summarizing produced some of the largest learning effects of any strategy studied</cite>, and <cite index="107-1">teaching students to summarize improves memory for what's read and works as a built-in check for comprehension</cite>. The two-sentence limit forces genuine identification of what matters most.

**Evidence classification:** Direct research support

**Sources:**
- Summarizing — Reading Rockets — https://www.readingrockets.org/classroom/classroom-strategies/summarizing — On summarizing's strong evidence base for comprehension and memory.
- How to Teach Summarizing, Part I — Shanahan on Literacy, 2019 — https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/how-to-teach-summarizing-part-i — Cites the National Reading Panel's finding that summarizing had the largest effects among comprehension strategies studied.

**Cautions:** None significant.

### 22. Practice the Ask

**Parent promise:** Before a hard conversation with a teacher, practice it together first — rehearsing what to say builds the confidence to actually say it.

4. Have them say it again, improved.

### 23. Debate at the Table

**Parent promise:** Pick a low-stakes, genuinely debatable question and take opposite sides for ten minutes — it's real practice at the reasoning language school essays and discussions require.

**Recommended ages:** 12–14

**Time:** 10–15 minutes

**Primary focus:** Explaining and thinking

**Secondary focus:** Speaking

**Setting:** Any setting; mealtime

**Materials:** Nothing needed

**Steps:**
1. Pick a genuinely debatable, low-stakes question (best season, best way to spend a free Saturday).
2. Assign sides — even if your teen disagrees with theirs.
3. Each person gives one reason, then responds to the other's reason.
4. End by asking, "What's the strongest point the other side made?"

**Example:** Debating whether it's better to do homework right after school or after a break, a teen argues one side, then has to name the best point their parent made for the other side.

**Parent phrase:** "What's the strongest point they made?"

**Home-language option:** Debate in whichever language allows for the sharpest reasoning — the structure of argument transfers.

**Starting variation:** State one reason for a position.

**Growing variation:** State a reason and respond to a counterargument.

**Extending variation:** Argue a position you don't personally hold, then explain the strongest point against it.

**Why it helps:** <cite index="86-1">Training students to use more explicit reasoning language during discussion has been shown to shift how they use language together and to improve performance on tests of reasoning</cite>. Taking an assigned side, rather than only their own position, pushes teens to consider and articulate more than one perspective.

**Evidence classification:** Adapted from a research-supported principle

**Sources:**
- Children's Talk and the Development of Reasoning in the Classroom — Mercer, British Educational Research Journal (Wiley), 1999 — https://bera-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0141192990250107 — Study on explicit reasoning-talk training improving children's language use and reasoning; original study population was slightly younger (9–10), so this activity adapts the underlying principle for teens.

**Cautions:** Choose genuinely low-stakes topics; avoid family conflict points or anything that could feel like a real disagreement rather than a game.

### 24. Accent Pride Check-in

# Lesson 1: Your Home Language Is a Superpower

## Website Lesson

**Continuing to use your strongest family language supports — not delays — your child's English development. Here's why, and what it can look like at home this week.**

### The big idea

The language your family already speaks well is not standing in the way of English. It's building the foundation English will stand on.

### Plain-language explanation

Every time your child has a rich conversation in a language they know deeply, they're doing important work: organizing thoughts, learning new ideas, connecting with family, and practicing how language itself works. That work transfers. A child who can explain, argue, wonder, and tell stories well in one language is building skills that carry into every other language they learn — including English. Researchers and teachers who work with multilingual children see this again and again: strong home language ability tends to go hand in hand with stronger English development over time, not weaker.

### A common concern

*"Should we speak more English at home, even when we can't express ourselves as naturally?"*

It's a reasonable question, and it comes from wanting to help. But a conversation where everyone can only say simple things is often less valuable than a rich conversation in the language your family knows best. Save your family's full expressiveness — your humor, your nuance, your real opinions — for the language that lets everyone bring it. English can still enter your home naturally, through books, shows, games, and your child's own interests, without needing to replace family conversation.

### Three practical examples

- **Discussing a family decision** — like where to go this weekend or how to handle a schedule conflict — in the language that lets everyone reason it through fully.
- **Explaining something difficult** — a science idea from homework, a confusing event in the news — using whichever language makes the explanation clearest.
- **Telling family stories** — about grandparents, where the family is from, how you met your partner — in the language those stories naturally live in.

### Try this this week

Have one rich family conversation in the language that lets everyone express their best ideas. Ask a question with no single correct answer — "What would you do if...?" or "What do you think will happen next in...?" — and let the conversation run.

### Say this to your child

> "Tell me what you think — in whichever language helps you explain it best."

### When to talk with the school

If you're ever unsure how your child's home language use fits with what they're learning at school, it's a great question to bring to a teacher or EAL specialist. They can tell you what they're seeing in the classroom and how home language conversation supports it.

Your family's language is already giving your child something no worksheet can. Keep using it, and keep it rich.

# Lesson 2: Talk That Grows Thinking

## Website Lesson

**Better family conversation isn't about asking more questions — it's about making room for your child to think and say more. A few small shifts can turn "fine" into a real conversation.**

### The big idea

The goal isn't more questions. It's better invitations — the kind that give your child something real to respond to.

### Plain-language explanation

Some questions close a conversation down almost as soon as they open it. "How was school?" is enormous and vague — most children, in any language, don't know where to start, so they default to "fine." Specific, low-pressure invitations work differently. They point to something concrete, they don't demand a single right answer, and they leave room for your child to think before responding. Over time, this kind of talk — where a child predicts, compares, explains, and reflects — builds not just conversation skills but the thinking skills that support reading, writing, and learning.

### A common concern

*"My child answers every question with 'fine,' 'nothing,' or 'I don't know.'"*

This is one of the most common things families notice, and it's rarely about your child having nothing to say. It's usually about the question being too big. A more specific, more genuine invitation almost always gets a fuller answer — and giving your child a few quiet seconds to respond, without filling the silence yourself, matters just as much as the question.

### Three practical examples

- Instead of "How was school?" try **"What made you laugh today?"** or **"What was easier today than yesterday?"**
- Ask your child to **"Show me something you worked on"** — a drawing, a notebook page, anything physical to talk about together.
- When your child gives a short answer, follow with **"Tell me more"** instead of moving to a new question — and consider sharing your own answer first to model what a fuller response can look like.

### Try this this week

Replace one yes-or-no question each day with a specific invitation. After you ask, count to five in your head before speaking again — the pause does real work.

### Say this to your child

> "There is no rush. I want to hear what you think."

### When to talk with the school

If your child is consistently quiet or gives very short answers about school across many conversations over weeks, it's worth mentioning to their teacher — not as a problem to fix, but as something you're both watching together.

Don't turn every conversation into a lesson. A little curiosity, offered warmly, goes further than any technique.

# Lesson 3: Read Together, Your Way

## Website Lesson

**You don't need to read English aloud perfectly to build your child's literacy. Books in any language, read in whatever way works for your family, build the same important skills.**

### The big idea

Reading together isn't about performing English correctly. It's about building shared meaning from a story, in whatever language gets you there.

### Plain-language explanation

Literacy grows from far more than sounding out words on a page. It grows from talking about pictures before you even start reading, predicting what happens next, retelling a story afterward, connecting it to real life, and simply enjoying a story together — through an audiobook, a read-aloud in your home language, or a book your child reads to you. All of these build comprehension, vocabulary, and a love of stories that transfers across languages, including into a child's English reading at school.

### A common concern

*"The school book is in English, and I'm worried I'll pronounce something incorrectly."*

This worry is common, and it's worth setting down. You have several good options that don't require perfect English pronunciation: invite your child to read aloud to you, listen to an audiobook together, or read the pictures and talk through the story's meaning in your home language. None of these are lesser choices — the goal is shared meaning, not a flawless performance.

### Three practical examples

- **A picture walk** before reading — look through the pictures together and guess what the story might be about.
- **Alternating pages** — you read one, your child reads the next, in whichever language or format works.
- **Retelling afterward** — ask your child to tell you the story again in your home language, or compare a character's experience to something in your family's life.

### Try this this week

Spend ten minutes with any book, in any language. Ask one question before reading ("What do you think this will be about?"), one during ("What do you think happens next?"), and one after ("What surprised you?").

### Say this to your child

> "What does this part make you think about?"

### When to talk with the school

If you'd like suggestions for books at your child's level, or want to understand what reading skills they're working on right now, your child's teacher or librarian is a great resource.

Every book you share together — in every language — is building the reader your child is becoming.

# Lesson 4: Why My Child May Be Quiet at School

## Website Lesson

**A child who talks freely at home but stays quiet at school isn't necessarily struggling. Quiet can still mean listening, watching, thinking, and learning.**

### The big idea

Silence isn't the opposite of participation. Sometimes it's how participation looks while a child is still gathering the language and confidence to speak up.

### Plain-language explanation

Learning in a second language takes real mental effort — following a lesson, understanding classmates, and finding the words to respond all happen at once. Many children go through a stretch where they're absorbing far more than they're producing out loud. This is a normal part of the process, not a sign that something is wrong. It becomes worth a closer look only if it continues for a long time, or if your child seems distressed rather than simply cautious.

### A common concern

*"My child speaks freely at home but barely speaks at school."*

This gap is common and usually makes sense: home is familiar, low-pressure, and uses a language your child fully controls. School asks them to think, listen, and respond in a language they're still building — in front of classmates. Quiet participation there often reflects effort, not disengagement.

### Three practical examples

- Notice when your child mentions something they **noticed or overheard** at school, even if they didn't speak up — that's active listening at work.
- Ask about a moment they **watched a classmate do something**, like a game or an activity, before trying it themselves.
- Celebrate small steps, like raising a hand once, without turning it into a bigger deal than it feels to your child.

### Try this this week

Ask your child to name one school situation that feels comfortable and one that feels difficult — without rushing to fix the difficult one right away. Just listen.

### Say this to your child

> "You do not have to answer quickly. Help me understand what it feels like."

### When to talk with the school

If your child's quietness continues for months with little change, or seems connected to worry or distress rather than simply building confidence, it's a good moment to check in with their teacher about what they're seeing in class.

Quiet is not the same as absent. Your child is likely working harder than it looks.

# Lesson 5: Social English and School English Are Different

## Website Lesson

**A child who chats confidently at recess may still need real support with the language of explaining, comparing, and writing. Both kinds of English matter, and they grow at different speeds.**

### The big idea

Sounding fluent in conversation and being ready for academic work in English are two different milestones — and the first one usually arrives well before the second.

### Plain-language explanation

The English children use to chat with friends, joke around, and navigate daily life tends to develop relatively quickly, often within a couple of years. The English needed to explain a science process, compare two characters in a book, or write a structured paragraph is a different, more demanding kind of language — and it typically takes much longer to build, even for children who sound completely comfortable socially. This isn't a sign anything is wrong; it's simply a longer, deeper kind of language learning.

### A common concern

*"My child sounds fluent, so why is schoolwork still difficult?"*

This gap surprises many families, and it's genuinely confusing when a child seems so at ease in English. Confident playground conversation and confident academic writing draw on different skills, and it's completely normal for one to outpace the other for years.

### Three practical examples

- Ask your child to **explain how something works** — a game's rules, a recipe step, a science idea — in any language, with drawings or gestures if helpful.
- Notice when schoolwork uses words like *compare*, *explain*, or *argue* — these carry more academic weight than everyday conversation.
- Let your child **show their thinking** through a mix of talk, drawing, and writing, rather than requiring polished English sentences right away.

### Try this this week

Ask your child to explain how or why something works — a game, a recipe, an idea from class — using any language, drawings, gestures, or examples that help.

### Say this to your child

> "Show me your thinking in the way that works best for you."

### When to talk with the school

If you're curious how your child's academic English is developing compared to their conversational English, ask the teacher — this is exactly the kind of distinction EAL teachers track closely, often using a framework called WIDA, which looks at language across listening, speaking, reading, and writing.

Fluent conversation is a real accomplishment. Academic language is simply the next mountain, and it's one every learner climbs at their own pace.

# Lesson 6: Mistakes Are Evidence of Learning

## Website Lesson

**Language mistakes often show a child is experimenting with more complex ideas, not falling behind. Knowing when to correct — and when to simply respond — makes a real difference.**

### The big idea

A grammar mistake is frequently a sign of ambition — a child reaching for a sentence more complex than the one they've already mastered.

### Plain-language explanation

Children learning a language, including their first one, go through predictable phases of "productive error" — saying things like "I goed" instead of "I went" because they've correctly noticed a pattern (add *-ed* for past tense) and are applying it a little too broadly. This isn't a bad habit forming; it's a sign of real grammatical reasoning at work. Constant correction can interrupt the flow of conversation and make a child hesitant to try harder sentences at all. Gentle modeling — repeating the idea back with the correct form, without stopping to explain the rule — usually helps more over time.

### A common concern

*"If I don't correct every error, will my child develop bad habits?"*

It's a natural worry, but research and classroom experience both point the other way: constant correction tends to make children more cautious, not more accurate, while calm modeling in the flow of real conversation supports steady improvement without the discouragement.

### Three practical examples

- Your child says "I goed to the park" — you respond, **"Oh, you went to the park? What did you do there?"** — modeling the correct form without commentary.
- During homework, respond to the **idea first**, then note one pattern gently, rather than marking every error.
- Praise the **attempt at a complex sentence**, even an imperfect one, over a simpler but fully correct one.

### Try this this week

Respond to meaning first. When your child makes a grammar mistake, repeat their sentence back naturally with the correct form, then continue the conversation — no stopping, no explanation needed.

### Say this to your child

> "I understand your idea. Tell me more."

### When to talk with the school

If you're wondering whether a specific pattern of errors is typical for your child's age and language stage, their teacher can offer helpful context — they see this across many children and can tell you what's expected at this point.

Mistakes mean your child is reaching further than before. That's exactly what learning looks like.

# Lesson 7: Homework Without Taking Over

## Website Lesson

**You can support your child's homework meaningfully without knowing every answer, understanding every instruction, or completing the work yourself.**

### The big idea

The most useful homework help often isn't about content at all — it's about helping your child organize what they already know how to do.

### Plain-language explanation

When homework is confusing, the instinct to jump in and solve it is understandable — but some of the most valuable support doesn't require understanding the English or the subject matter deeply. Clarifying what a task is actually asking, breaking a big assignment into smaller steps, and asking your child to explain what they already understand all help far more than providing answers, and they build independence your child will need long after this one assignment is finished.

### A common concern

*"How can I help when I don't understand the English or the assignment?"*

This is one of the most common homework worries families share, and the good news is that understanding the content isn't actually the most important part of your role here. Your questions matter more than your answers.

### Three practical examples

- **Clarify the task together** — read the instructions aloud and ask, "What do you think this is asking you to do?"
- **Break it into steps** — "What's the very first thing you need to do?" rather than looking at the whole assignment at once.
- **Ask your child to show you** what they already understand before either of you tries to solve the hard part.

### Try this this week

Use three questions for one homework session: *What is the task? What do you understand? What is your next step?* Then step back and let your child work.

### Say this to your child

> "Show me where you feel confident and where you feel stuck."

### When to talk with the school

If your child is consistently unable to understand homework instructions even after this kind of support, that's worth flagging to the teacher — it may mean the instructions need to be clearer or the task needs adjusting.

You don't need every answer. You need the right questions, and you already have those.

# Lesson 8: Vocabulary Grows Through Real Life

## Website Lesson

**Children build the vocabulary that actually sticks through real experiences and repeated, natural use — not through memorized word lists.**

### The big idea

A word learned while doing something real — cooking, shopping, playing — tends to stay. A word memorized from a list often doesn't.

### Plain-language explanation

Vocabulary grows strongest when a word is connected to an experience, used more than once, and tied to something your child actually cares about. Cooking together, running errands, playing a game, or talking through a hobby all naturally introduce and repeat useful words in context — which is far more effective than isolated memorization, and requires no special materials or English fluency from you.

### A common concern

*"Should my child memorize more English words?"*

Word lists have their place, but they rarely build the kind of vocabulary that shows up naturally in speech and writing. Words encountered repeatedly during real activities, in a meaningful context, tend to stick far better.

### Three practical examples

- **Cooking together** — naming ingredients, actions ("chop," "stir," "measure"), and using them again the next time you cook.
- **Shopping** — comparing prices, sizes, or quantities out loud, in whatever language works, then introducing the English word if useful.
- **A hobby or game** — talking through the specific vocabulary of something your child loves, from card games to soccer to drawing.

### Try this this week

Choose five useful words from one family activity — cooking, a game, an errand — and use them naturally several times over the week, without turning it into a quiz.

### Say this to your child

> "What other word could describe this?"

### When to talk with the school

If you're curious what vocabulary your child is currently working on in class, ask the teacher — you can often reinforce those exact words at home through everyday activities.

Every trip to the market, every game, every recipe is quietly building your child's vocabulary. No list required.

# Lesson 9: Helping Children Become Stronger Writers

## Website Lesson

**Good writing starts with ideas, talk, and planning — not perfect spelling. You can help your child become a stronger writer without rewriting a single sentence.**

### The big idea

The hardest part of writing usually isn't the writing itself — it's figuring out what to say. That's exactly where you can help most.

### Plain-language explanation

Before a single word goes on the page, strong writers do a lot of invisible work: generating ideas, deciding what matters most, and organizing their thoughts. Talking through an idea out loud, drawing it out, or simply naming a few key points before writing begins can make the actual writing far less overwhelming. None of this requires correcting grammar or rewriting your child's sentences — your job is to help ideas take shape, not to polish the final product.

### A common concern

*"My child says they have nothing to write."*

This is almost never really true — it usually means the ideas haven't been shaped yet. Talking it through first, out loud, in any language, is often all it takes to unlock a starting point.

### Three practical examples

- **Tell it before you write it** — ask your child to tell you the story or explanation out loud first, in whichever language is easiest.
- **Draw before writing** — a quick sketch of the scene or idea can make the writing that follows much easier.
- **Name three key ideas** together before your child starts, so they know where the piece is headed.

### Try this this week

Ask your child to tell their story or idea out loud before writing. Help them name three key points they want to include, then let them write independently.

### Say this to your child

> "Which part of your idea matters most?"

### When to talk with the school

If your child consistently struggles to generate any ideas even after talking things through, mention it to the teacher — they can share what supports are used in class and whether additional strategies might help.

You don't need to fix a single sentence to help your child write better. Helping the ideas take shape is enough.

# Lesson 10: Understanding Language Progress Without Comparison

## Website Lesson

**Language growth is uneven and individual — and it's better understood through your own child's progress over time than by comparing them to classmates.**

### The big idea

The most useful measure of your child's language growth isn't another child. It's your child, a few months ago.

### Plain-language explanation

Listening, speaking, reading, and writing don't all develop at the same pace — a child might read confidently while speaking is still developing, or the reverse. Progress also varies widely between children depending on age, personality, prior schooling, and how many languages they're already navigating. Comparing your child to a classmate rarely gives useful information, because the comparison isn't measuring the same starting point or the same path. Many schools use a framework called WIDA to track growth across these different language areas — not as a label, but as one useful source of information among several.

### A common concern

*"Why is another child learning English faster?"*

This comparison is natural, but it's rarely apples to apples — different children arrive with different language backgrounds, ages, and experiences, and progress in different areas at different speeds. Your child's own trajectory is the meaningful one to watch.

### Three practical examples

- Keep a **simple mental note** of something your child couldn't do a few months ago that they can do now.
- Ask the teacher about your child's **own growth over time**, rather than how they compare to the class.
- Celebrate a **specific improvement** — a longer sentence, a new word used correctly — rather than a vague sense of "better" or "worse."

### Try this this week

Notice one thing your child can do now that was difficult earlier in the year — a phrase, a sentence structure, a new confidence in a certain situation — and mention it to them.

### Say this to your child

> "I noticed something you can do now that you could not do before."

### When to talk with the school

If you want a clearer picture of your child's progress, ask the teacher to walk you through how they're doing across listening, speaking, reading, and writing — this kind of breakdown is often more useful than any single score or comparison.

Your child's language is growing on its own timeline. That timeline is the one worth watching.

# Lesson 11: Preparing for a Productive Teacher Meeting

## Website Lesson

**You don't need dozens of questions for a useful teacher meeting — two or three focused ones, chosen in advance, can tell you what you actually need to know.**

### The big idea

A short list of the right questions gets you further than a long list of general ones, especially when the meeting is in a language that isn't your strongest.

### Plain-language explanation

Teacher meetings can feel overwhelming, particularly when you're navigating them in English. Going in with two or three specific questions — about your child's strengths, participation, language support, or a concrete example of their work — helps you leave with something useful, rather than a general impression. It also helps to decide beforehand on one thing you most want the teacher to understand about your child.

### A common concern

*"I'm not sure what to ask, especially when the meeting is in English."*

This is one of the most common worries families share, and preparing a short, specific list in advance solves most of it — you don't need to think on your feet if you've already decided what matters most.

### Three practical examples

- Ask to **see an example of your child's work**, so you have something concrete to discuss rather than general impressions.
- Ask **one question about strengths** and **one about a next step**, so the meeting covers both.
- Decide beforehand on **one thing about your child** you want the teacher to know — a strength, an interest, a recent change at home — and share it early in the meeting.

### Try this this week

Choose three questions in advance for your next teacher meeting, and write down one thing you want the teacher to understand about your child before you go in.

### Say this to your school

> "Could you show me an example of my child's work?"

### When to talk with the school

If you'd like the meeting conducted with an interpreter or translated materials, most schools can arrange this — it's worth asking ahead of time so you can participate fully.

A short, focused list of questions is often worth more than an hour of general conversation. You know what matters most to your family — bring that in with you.

# Lesson 12: Protecting Confidence and Belonging

## Website Lesson

**Children learn language more easily when they feel their names, languages, and experiences truly belong — confidence and belonging aren't a side note to language learning, they're the foundation of it.**

### The big idea

A child who feels embarrassed by their accent or their home language is fighting two battles at once. Protecting belonging makes room for language to grow.

### Plain-language explanation

Learning a new language while also adjusting to a new culture, school, or country asks a lot of a child emotionally, not just linguistically. When a child feels self-conscious about an accent, hesitant to use their home language in public, or unsure whether their background fits in, that discomfort can quietly get in the way of the confidence language learning needs. Protecting your child's sense of belonging — in their name, their languages, their story — isn't separate from supporting their English. It's part of the same work.

### A common concern

*"My child is embarrassed to use our family language or worried about their accent."*

This is a painful thing to watch, and it's more common than many families realize. It often eases with time and reassurance, especially when a child sees their languages and background treated as something valuable, not something to hide.

### Three practical examples

- **Share a family story** about language, migration, or communicating across cultures — normalizing the experience of navigating more than one world.
- **Use your child's name correctly and confidently** in every setting, and encourage others to do the same.
- **Notice and name a moment of pride** — something your child did that drew on their multilingual background, even something small.

### Try this this week

Share one family story about learning something new, moving somewhere unfamiliar, communicating across languages, or making a mistake and figuring it out.

### Say this to your child

> "Every language you know is part of who you are."

### When to talk with the school

If your child seems consistently anxious, withdrawn, or distressed about belonging at school — not just occasionally self-conscious — it's worth raising with their teacher or school counselor, who can offer support beyond what a single conversation at home can address.

Confidence and language grow together. Protecting one protects the other.

# Optional Lesson 13: Screens, Games, and English Learning

## Website Lesson

**Digital media can genuinely support language learning — but it depends far more on how it's used than on how much time it takes.**

### The big idea

The question isn't "how much screen time" — it's "how connected is that screen time to talk, thinking, and real interaction."

### Plain-language explanation

Screens aren't automatically good or bad for language learning. A show watched together, paused to talk about, or discussed afterward builds language in a way that passive, uninterrupted watching doesn't. Games that involve reading, problem-solving, or communicating with others can build real vocabulary and comprehension. Subtitles — in English or your home language — can support reading without adding pressure. What matters most is whether the screen time creates opportunities for interaction and meaning-making, and whether it fits within boundaries that work for your family.

### A common concern

*"Is screen time helping or hurting my child's English?"*

Neither answer is fully right on its own — it depends on the content and how your family engages with it. Co-viewing, talking about what's happening, and choosing content connected to your child's real interests make screen time far more likely to help.

### Three practical examples

- **Watch together and pause to talk** — "What do you think happens next?" or "Why do you think she did that?"
- **Try subtitles** — in English to support reading, or in your home language to support understanding, depending on what helps most right now.
- **Choose games that involve reading or communication**, and ask your child to tell you about strategies or storylines afterward.

### Try this this week

Watch or play something with your child, and pause once to ask a genuine question about what's happening — a prediction, an opinion, or a connection to real life.

### Say this to your child

> "What do you think is going to happen next?"

### When to talk with the school

If you're curious whether specific apps or programs your child uses at home align with what they're learning at school, it's a reasonable question to ask their teacher.

Screens aren't the enemy of language learning, and they're not a shortcut either. What happens around the screen is what counts.

# Optional Lesson 14: When a Language Concern May Need More Support

## Website Lesson

**Learning multiple languages does not cause a language disorder — but children can experience learning or communication difficulties in any language, and it's worth knowing when to ask for more support.**

### The big idea

Multilingualism isn't the cause of a language difficulty. If something feels genuinely different from typical language learning, the home language your child knows best is actually the clearest place to look for answers.

### Plain-language explanation

It's completely normal for language learning to be uneven, gradual, and sometimes frustrating — that alone isn't cause for concern. But occasionally, a child experiences a difficulty that shows up across all of their languages, not just English — for example, struggling to find words, follow directions, or communicate clearly even in the language they know best. Because so much variation is normal, it can be hard to tell the difference between typical multilingual development and something that may need more support. This is not something to diagnose at home, and it's not something a single lesson can determine — it calls for a conversation with your child's school and, if needed, a qualified professional.

### A common concern

*"Could learning several languages be causing my child's difficulties?"*

Research consistently shows that learning multiple languages does not cause language disorders or delays. If a genuine difficulty exists, it will generally show up across all of a child's languages, not because of the number of languages, but separately from it.

### Three practical examples

- **Notice patterns across languages** — does a difficulty with finding words or following directions show up in your home language too, not just English?
- **Keep simple notes** — a few examples over time are more useful to a specialist than a single moment.
- **Talk with people who know your child in different settings** — a teacher, a family member — to see whether observations match across contexts.

### Try this this week

If you have a genuine concern, write down two or three specific examples of what you've noticed, including whether it happens in your home language as well as English. This will be useful if you decide to talk with the school.

### Say this to your child

> "I'm here to help you, and we'll figure this out together."

### When to talk with the school

If you notice a pattern of difficulty that shows up across your child's languages — not just English — or that feels different from the everyday ups and downs of learning, bring it to your child's teacher or school support team. They can help determine appropriate next steps, which may include further evaluation by a qualified professional. This lesson cannot and does not diagnose any condition.

Trust what you notice about your child across all their languages. Asking for support early is a sign of good parenting, not something to worry about.
